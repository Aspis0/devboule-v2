Most generated pages are a known type drawn from memory, and a remembered pricing page or settings screen is the average of the training data. Naming real examples first makes the convention checkable. NN/g's fourth usability heuristic states Jakob's Law: people spend most of their time in other products, and those set their expectations ([NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)).

**Search when the type is conventional.** Pricing, changelog, settings, onboarding, checkout, sign-in, account, search results: types whose information order and covered states are settled. Search when the brief names one and the project does not supply it; skip it for a single button or field, for an experimental layout with no convention yet, and whenever the attached folder already fixes the pattern. The project wins over the web.

**Search for pages, not for advice.** Use WebSearch and WebFetch on live examples of the same type — a comparable product's pricing page, a shipped app's changelog. An article titled "how to design a pricing page" summarises the same conventions second-hand. Read the whole page, not the first screen. Two or three examples separate a convention from one product's choice.

**Carry the structure, leave the surface.** Take the skeleton: what is above the fold, the order of sections, the question each answers, what the page omits, which states exist (trial, expired, no results), where the primary action sits. Leave colours, type, brand marks, imagery and every sentence of their copy. A convention is the shape; the skin belongs to the page you read.

**Draw the line at reproduction.** Reading an arrangement to understand it is reference; rebuilding that page's layout and words is copying. Keep what several examples share and discard what only one does — a choice that survives because one page made it is that product's expression, not a convention. A working rule, not legal advice.

**Name what you read.** When a page informs a choice, name it in the reply beside the choice, so the user can check it; an attribution-free claim of inspiration is not verifiable and does not count. If no example was found or the web tools are unavailable, say so once and fall back to the craft sections. Examples show conventions, not requirements.
